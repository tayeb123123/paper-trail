How to choose a commuter bike you'll actually ride

by M. Okafor · 9 min read

Most advice about bikes is written for racers. Commuters want something else: a bike that is fast enough to beat the bus, cheap enough that leaving it at a rack doesn't hurt, and reliable enough to start every morning without a ritual. Those three goals pull in different directions, and every bike is a compromise between them.

Speed is mostly about you

A quick bike is a light bike, but only up to a point. Past about eleven kilograms the difference on a flat city street is minutes a year, not seconds a day. Tyre pressure and a well-adjusted chain matter more. If you want to be rapid on the way to work, look at your route before you look at the frame.

What does make a difference is gearing. A single-speed is quick off the line and nearly maintenance-free, which is why couriers love them. A hub gear is slower to accelerate but dependable in rain, salt and neglect.

The best bike is the one that is still working in February.

Where the money goes

An inexpensive bike is not the same as a cheap one. Budget bikes save money on the parts you touch most: brakes, shifters, saddle. A second-hand steel frame with new brake pads is often both more affordable and more trustworthy than a new bike at the same price.

Here is the tension in one paragraph: the fast option is rarely the cheap one, and the cheap option is rarely the reliable one. Speedy, inexpensive and dependable is the corner of the triangle every commuter is trying to reach — and the honest answer is that a sturdy, quick, affordable bike exists, but it is usually a few years old.

Reliability you can see

Look for sealed bearings, a chain guard and mudguard mounts. A dependable bike has boring parts that any shop can replace. Exotic components are a fast way to spend a weekend waiting for a delivery.

Finally: buy lights that charge over USB, a lock that weighs more than you'd like, and a bell. None of them make the bike faster, but all of them make you more likely to ride it.
